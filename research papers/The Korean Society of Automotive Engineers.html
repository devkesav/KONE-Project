The Abstract

Batteries and supercapacitors are gaining attention as energy sources for electric vehicles due to their complementary characteristics in energy density and power density. To effectively utilize the advantages of each energy source, a hybrid energy management system that can distribute the required power between batteries and supercapacitors is essential. This paper proposed a reinforcement learning-based hybrid energy management system and a reward function that enhances a battery’s state of health (SOH). Simulation results demonstrate that the suggested energy management system (EMS) maintains higher battery SOH than existing EMSs.

1. Introduction

Electric vehicles (EVs) use batteries as the main energy source, and in recent years, various energy sources such as supercapacitors and fuel cells have been used together to form hybrid energy packs, and research is actively underway to increase energy efficiency and battery life.1 - 44)

Lithium-ion batteries have a higher power density and energy density than other types of batteries, which are widely used in electric vehicles, but there is a problem that the power density is low compared to supercapacitors and relatively short-lived. On the contrary, there is a problem that the supercapacitor has a high power density and a short charging time, but has a lower energy density compared to the battery. However, unlike batteries, electrochemical reactions do not occur, so the service life is long, and the reaction speed is short, which is suitable for the rapid power supply required for acceleration. As such, batteries and supercapacitors have complementary properties, so configuring a hybrid energy pack with two energy sources can be useful in electric vehicles. However, to take advantage of the advantages of each energy source that makes up a hybrid energy pack, we need a hybrid energy management system (HEMS) that can efficiently distribute the load of each energy source when driving. There are five main types of load distribution strategies in energy management 5)systems: rule-based strategy, optimization-based strategy, and learning-based strategy.

A rule-based strategy is a heuristic control technique defined as a set of “if-then” procedures. They can be subdivided internally into deterministic-based strategies and fuzzy sogic-based strategies. A deterministic strategy is a technique that always operates on a fixed rule. A fuzzy logic-based strategy, unlike binary logic, is a technique that deals with the degree of truth, and operates on the basis of the degree to which a particular element belongs to a certain set. Rule-based strategies do not guarantee optimal results, but they are often used in commercial vehicles because they are small in computational volume and simple implementation.

An optimization-based strategy is a control technique that can achieve global optimal harm. Examples include Dynamic Programming (DP). Dynamic programming solves a given problem by dividing it into several small problems first, and using the results to find the best solution for the whole problem. However, the optimization-based strategy requires future information such as the overall driving cycle and power consumption profile, and it cannot be used as a real-time control method because it requires a large amount of computation.

A learning-based strategy is a technique that uses an artificial intelligence model. A prime example is reinforcement learning, which is one of the methods of machine learning. Reinforcement learning is the best learning for an agent to interact with the environment and to learn the best action to choose from in a particular state. The Environment provides rewards as a feedback that is an indicator of how appropriate an Agent's Action is. Agents can learn actions that maximize rewards according to the State entered each time, and learns a load-sharing strategy that is close to optimal.

This paper proposes a battery-supercapacitor hybrid energy management system using one of the learning-based strategies, Reinforcement learning-based strategy. By designing a reward function for efficient use of a supercapacitor, the Agent can learn a load distribution strategy to maximize the reward value, thereby increasing battery life.

2. Suggested Hybrid Energy Management System

Fig. 1 is the overall overview of the proposed reinforcement learning-based hybrid energy management system. The Soft Actor Critic (SAC) Agent, located inside EMS, is a state that receives the status of the current energy pack and the required power for driving. The Actor neural network inside the SAC Agent prints the action, which is the ratio that the supercapacitor will be charged at the required power, according to the input state, and the Critic neural network enters the state and Action, and evaluates the Q-value to evaluate how appropriate the Action output by the Actor neural network is. According to the action, after calculating the power for each energy source, the two-way DC-DC converter connected to each energy source is controlled to provide power to the motor or recover the power by regenerative braking. In this way, each energy source is connected to a two-way DC-DC converter, so that the required power generated during acceleration or the power obtained through the regenerative braking can be distributed to the battery and the supercapacitor.

2.2 Strengthening learning model
2.2.1 Soft Actor Critic Agent

SAC agent is a reinforcement learning algorithm that focuses on learning policy in continuous state and action. SAC is an actor and Critic neural network-based reinforcement learning algorithm, and the Actor neural network approximates Agent's action when given a state, and the Critic neural network approximates a Q-value when given a state and action. In this way, the actor and Critic interact and the agent can identify actions that can earn high rewards from the environment. The best feature of SAC is the addition of entropy to learn, which leads the Agent to explore more actions. SAC can learn from achieving high rewards while avoiding converging problems with local optimal results.

2.2.3 Compensation Functions

Fig. 2 is a reward function used for Agent learning. The reward function uses a total of five reward items. Line 1 to 3 is a reward for the supercapacitor discharge, and is compensated when the SOC of the supercapacitor is within the available range and the differential value of P D is greater than 0, i.e., when the required power amount is increasing. The reward given here is the value of multiplying P D and R SC, and the higher the amount of supercapacitor subsidized when the required power is high, the higher the reward can be obtained. Fig. 3 is a graph that visualizes the reward calculated through line 3, and it can be seen that both P D and R SC obtain higher reward values as they are closer to 1. Even if the R SC is the same, the higher the P D, the higher the P D, the more effective the agent will learn the strategy of assisting the battery. Line 5 to 7 is a reward for supercapacitor charging. When P D is negative, that is, when the energy pack is charged through the regenerative braking from the motor, it is rewarded. At this time, R SC is used as a reward value to grant higher rewards to the supercapacitor charge. Lines 9 to 11 and lines 13 to 15 are penalties for supercapacitor overdischarge and overcharge, respectively, reducing rewards by R SC. The agent can learn to minimize these penalties to assist the battery while keeping the SOC of the supercapacitor within the available range. Finally, lines 17 to 19 are given as the reward value for the SOC of the supercapacitor as the base reward. This is responsible for ensuring that the agent maintains the SOC of the supercapacitor enough to assist the battery at any time. However, if the agent receives the basic reward and overcharge/overcharge penalty at the same time, it is more likely that the agent will learn the wrong strategy by canceling the penalty. To prevent this, the basic reward is given only when no penalty is granted. Agent learns how to use Supercapacitors through rewards and penalties based on various conditions, such as above, to obtain high rewards.

3.1 Driving Cycle Profile

The paper used four standard driving cycles, including FTP72, WLTP Class 1, 2, and 3, for the analysis of Agent's learning and performance. Fig. 4 is the speed profile of each driving cycle, and Table 4 is a parameter that can indicate the output of the driving cycle, the average speed and the maximum speed, and the average power charge measured in the simulation and the average power charge. The FTP72 accelerates to an average of 31.4 km/h and a maximum of 91.2 km/h. WLTP Class 1 accelerates to an average of 28.5 km/h, up to 64 km/h, and WLTP Class 2 accelerates to an average of 35.7 km/h and up to 85 km/h. For this purpose, WLTP Class 3 cycles, which include low-speed, medium-speed, and high-speed driving, and which are most active in power consumption and charging are used for Agent learning, and each cycle is simulated to analyze performance.

3.3 Performance Comparison
3.3.1 Analysis of Agent's Distribution Strategy

Fig. 6 is the result of simulating the WLTP Class 3 running cycle used for Agent learning. (a) is a power distribution graph that indicates the required power and the power consumed by the supercapacitor, and (b) represents the SOC change of each energy source according to the power distribution. Basically, when the required power increased, the agent used the power of the supercapacitor to assist the battery, and used most of the energy obtained by the regenerative braking to charge the supercapacitor. After 1,500 seconds of driving, the required power increases rapidly, but the supercapacitor provides less power than the first half of the ride, as the supercapacitor energy pack SOC has already been reduced by 70%. The agent has satisfied the minimum SOC constraints of the supercapacitor by controlling the power supply according to the current SOC status of the supercapacitor energy pack or charging it through regenerative braking, indicating that the agent has learned the intent of the compensation function well.

4. Conclusion

This paper proposes a battery-supercapacitor hybrid energy management system based on reinforcement learning. First of all, by designing the compensation and penalty according to the charge and discharge of the supercapacitor, the reinforcement learning agent learned a load-sharing strategy that can increase the battery life. After the training, the simulation was carried out using several driving cycles to compare the performance of the EMS proposed with the existing EMS. EMS used the power obtained through regenerative braking to charge the supercapacitor to maintain the minimum SOC of the supercapacitor, and the battery was auxiliaryed when accelerating, reducing the average power consumption of the battery. The proposed EMS has similar performance to existing EMS in all driving cycles, satisfying the minimum SOC constraints of the Supercapacitor. In addition, the average power consumption of the supercapacitor was the highest in average value, and the battery SOH was also the highest.
